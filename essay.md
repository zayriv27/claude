# At the Limit: What Sartre and Tolstoy Think a Human Being Actually Is

There is a moment near the end of "The Wall" where Pablo Ibbieta, who has spent the entire story refusing to give up Ramon Gris's location, decides to send his interrogators on a wild goose chase: he tells them Ramon is hiding in the cemetery. It is almost a joke, a last act of contempt from a man who expects to die in a few hours anyway. Except Ramon has moved. He is at his cousin's house, near the cemetery. The Fascists find him and shoot him. Pablo survives. His "heroic" refusal killed the man he refused to betray.

Sartre does this on purpose, and it is worth sitting with how genuinely unkind it is. The whole story sets up Pablo as someone making a principled stand, and then the stand turns out to be meaningless in every direction. He didn't save himself, didn't protect Ramon, and didn't even get the dignity of dying for something. The wall never stops being the wall. What changes is everything Pablo thought the wall meant.

Tolstoy's Pahom doesn't get the wall. He gets open land, an unlimited horizon, a deal so fair it seems impossible to lose: walk a circuit of Bashkir territory before sundown and it's yours. He starts walking. He keeps walking. He sees good land to the right and adds it to his circuit. He sees a stream and bends toward it. By midday he is so far from the starting point that running back becomes the only option, and he runs until his lungs fill with blood and he dies crossing the finish line. His servant buries him in a hole six feet long. The story's title is its thesis.

Both men hit a limit. Pablo hits his at a prison wall, with interrogators on the other side. Pahom hits his when his body runs out. The question both authors are asking, and the one this essay is going to try to answer, is what exactly gets revealed at that limit. Is it something biological, some fixed human nature that circumstances uncover? Or is it the choices the person made to get there?

## The Body Problem

Sartre is almost clinical about what happens to Pablo's body the night before his execution. His hands shake. He sweats through his clothes. He can't get warm. A Belgian doctor observes the prisoners and takes notes with the detached interest of someone studying a specimen. Pablo recognizes that the doctor's notes are accurate. His body is behaving like a body that knows it will die, and it doesn't ask Pablo's permission to do this.

This is the most disturbing part of the story for me, more than the execution itself. Pablo has spent years building an identity around political commitment. He is a resistant, he believes things, he has relationships that matter to him. None of it survives contact with his own involuntary fear response. He thinks about Concha, the woman he loves, and feels nothing. "I was unable to feel love for her," he says, "I would have liked to feel something. But I couldn't" (Sartre 12). His body has gone ahead without him.

In Sartre's philosophical framework, this is what he calls "facticity," the raw given-ness of existence, the biological facts you didn't choose and can't reason with. Your body is facticity. Your past is facticity. The problem Sartre identifies in his broader work is that people confuse facticity with identity, that they treat their circumstances as their essence rather than as the material they have to work with. Pablo has been doing this his whole life, treating his convictions as something he simply has rather than something he keeps choosing. The night before his death, the convictions evaporate, and what's left is a shaking body and the decision about what to do with it.

Pahom's body is a different kind of problem. His body isn't failing him. It's serving him, right up until it can't. He is physically capable of walking a large circuit of land. His body does what he asks. The problem is that what he asks keeps expanding. Tolstoy wrote this story in 1886, during a period when Russian peasants were for the first time navigating land as a commodity rather than as a fixed condition of life. The Emancipation Reform of 1861 had freed the serfs but left the question of land ownership genuinely unstable. Pahom isn't a fable of generic greed; he's a specific portrait of what happens to a person when accumulation becomes possible for the first time. The want isn't irrational given his context. It just has no internal stop (Maude 214).

What both bodies share is that they tell the truth. Pablo's body tells the truth about fear before his mind catches up. Pahom's body tells the truth about cost when his mind has stopped counting. The biological layer in both stories is not where meaning lives; it's where the consequences of choices finally become undeniable.

## What the Existentialist Reading Actually Requires

"Existence precedes essence" is the line students usually cite in a paper like this, and it's the right line, but it's easy to use it wrong. What Sartre means is not that humans are unusually flexible or that we can reinvent ourselves at will. He means something starker: there is no fixed human nature that came first, no essence you were born with that your life is supposed to express. You are the accumulation of your choices, nothing more. This is terrifying because it means you can't appeal to anything outside yourself, not God, not biology, not your social role, to explain or justify what you do. You chose it. You keep choosing it. Even refusing to choose is a choice.

Pablo's refusal to give up Ramon is a real choice in this sense. He is not following a rule or honoring an instinct. He is, in the specific circumstances of that night, deciding who he is going to be. The existentialist point is that this is all identity ever is. It's just usually invisible because the stakes are low enough that the choice doesn't register as a choice. The wall makes the stakes undeniable.

But here's where Tolstoy complicates things in a way that doesn't get discussed enough: Pahom is also making choices. Dozens of them. Every corner where he might turn back, he doesn't. These are not coerced decisions. Nobody is threatening him. The Bashkirs are sitting at the starting point waiting cheerfully. He could stop at any point and walk a reasonable circuit and live. He doesn't, but he could. Sartre would call this bad faith: acting as though you have no choice when you do, treating your desire as a force of nature rather than a decision you keep remaking. Pahom never once thinks "I could turn around now." He runs like a man who has forgotten that the running was optional.

The difference between the two characters is not that one is free and one is not. It's that Pablo knows he is choosing, even when the choosing doesn't accomplish anything, and Pahom doesn't know. Or won't look. That gap is where both authors locate the definition of the human. Not in biology, which just fails eventually. Not in some abstract essence. In the quality of awareness a person brings to the choices they can't avoid making.

## What Remains

Neither story ends well. Pablo survives the night but has watched everything he thought he was dissolve by morning. Pahom wins the land and a six-foot grave. Tolstoy's ending is almost too neat; the story knows it's making a point. Sartre's ending is genuinely ugly, in a way that feels more honest. The joke is on Pablo, and on the reader who was rooting for him.

What both writers leave you with is something close to the same argument: the boundary doesn't determine what you are; it just makes what you already were visible. Pahom was always running. He just needed a race to prove it. Pablo was always defiant. The defiance just needed a wall to show itself against. Whether that counts as defining human nature or just revealing a particular person's nature is a question I don't think either author settles. Sartre would resist any answer that sounds like "this is what all humans are." Tolstoy seems to believe there's a universal lesson in Pahom's grave.

Maybe the honest answer is that the two stories together make a case that humanity is defined by how conscious we are of our own choosing. Pahom has freedom and spends it without noticing. Pablo has almost no freedom left and uses every scrap of it deliberately, even when it kills Ramon, even when it turns out to mean nothing. Living on automatic versus choosing with your eyes open. That might be the closest either author gets to saying what a human being actually is.

It's not a comfortable definition. It doesn't promise anything. But it's the one the wall forces you to accept.
